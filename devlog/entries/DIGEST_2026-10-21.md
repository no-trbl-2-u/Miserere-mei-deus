# 2026-10-21 — The review pipeline learns to merge itself

> Two nights of mostly behind-the-scenes work. The project's own pull

> clean — a human only has to step in when something looks wrong. Thirty
> new hazard-puzzle candidates were authored and loaded onto the

## [infra] The project's own pull requests can now merge themselves

**What:** Every automated pull request the project opens now gets reviewed
by one or more focused AI readers — each assigned only the files and
modules its diff actually touches — instead of a single generalist pass
over everything. A pull request that is not part of the plan itself (the
project's own build-plan or playbook documents) and whose review comes
back clean now merges on its own, with no person needed. A pull request
whose review comes back with problems gets one automatic corrective pass
on its own branch before being handed back for another look. Two small
bugs in the plumbing that supports this — a circular import that crashed
every review, and a flaky end-to-end test that occasionally failed for
reasons unrelated to the change under review — were also found and fixed.

**Why:** a replay against four deliberately planted bugs found the new,
more focused review setup caught all four, while the old single-reviewer
setup caught three and missed one entirely (a bug in how two pieces of
game logic interact with each other, the kind a reviewer skimming the
whole diff is most likely to miss). Letting a clean review merge on its
own removes the project's single biggest reported bottleneck: work sitting
finished and reviewed, but waiting on a person's attention just to land.

## [content] Thirty candidate hazard puzzles, ready for your review

**What:** Thirty new hand-authored puzzle layouts for the hazard
mini-game were written — ten rated easy, ten medium, ten hard — spread
across all four of Act 1's regions, each reusing a real hazard's title and
card text so none of them feel invented out of nowhere. Every one of the
thirty was checked by the puzzle solver to make sure it actually asks the
player to think: none can be won by playing any card in any order, and
none demands exactly one path with no real choice. They're now loaded
onto the developer-only puzzle board in three labelled groups (by
difficulty), each playable on its own timer, so they can be played and
judged one at a time instead of only read as data.

**Why:** the puzzle mechanic can't be tuned or trusted at scale from a
handful of hand-picked examples — this is the pool the next step (you
playing each of the thirty, timed, to find out which ones actually feel
easy, medium or hard) needs to exist before it can happen. That next step
is tracked in the project's deadline list, due 2026-11-02.

## [mechanics] The puzzle's paid cards get real rules, and a test catches a worse copy

**What:** Hazard-puzzle cards that cost a die to use no longer get a free,
made-up default for what that die gives back — every card now states its
own numbers for "paid" and "unpaid" play, and a card can gain an extra
effect specifically for being paid that it doesn't get otherwise. A new
automatic check compares every card against every other card in the same
pool and flags any card that is simply worse in every situation than
another already in the pool — never fewer ways to win, sometimes more —
by name, so a bad trade can't quietly sit in the puzzle's card list
unnoticed.

**Why:** an earlier rule meant a player could be asked to pay a real cost
(a die, or progress on the card) for nothing but what every card already
got for free — a bad trade by design. The project's own standing rule now
is: if a card costs you something extra, it has to give you something
real back, and this is the test that holds that rule to its word going
forward, not just today.

**Commits:** `814cd6872`, `9ff95ae85` (phases HP1c2, HP1d; PRs #639, #647)

**No capture:** a rules and data change inside a developer-only puzzle
pool; no pixel changes.

## [mechanics] The puzzle board's dice roll and animate like the real thing

**What:** Picking a route on the developer-only puzzle board now plays
the same dice-rolling animation a real hazard encounter uses, landing on
the puzzle's own fixed dice faces instead of appearing instantly. Rerolling
a die now replays that same tumble in place, and gaining an extra die
mid-puzzle now arrives the same way a real hazard's bonus die does — a
small conjured animation rather than just appearing in the hand. None of
this changes when the puzzle's countdown clock starts: it still holds at
full until the roll finishes, so the animation never costs the player
time.

**Why:** the puzzle was deliberately built on a simplified, throwaway
screen first so its rules could be nailed down without a real screen's
complexity getting in the way. Now that the rules are settling, each
remaining piece of the real hazard's presentation gets ported over one at
a time — this is the dice half of that move; a visual-regression tool
caught one place the animation briefly outran its own capture, fixed in
the same work.

## [balance] Measuring the hazard-puzzle cards: an audit, five numbers, and a move log

**What:** Every card the hazard deck and the puzzle pool can draw on now
gets a written audit of what it actually does inside a puzzle (what the
puzzle ignores about it, whether it behaves like another card, and a
keep-or-cut recommendation with a reason) in a new report. The puzzle
solver now also reports five numbers for every puzzle it solves — how many
ways it can be won, how big a head start the opening move gives, how many
near-misses exist, the shortest possible win, and how much the outcome
depends on the dice draw — meant to eventually define what "easy,"
"medium" and "hard" actually mean in numbers rather than a guess. A new
developer-only move log records every action a test-solve makes, with a
timestamp for each, down to the moment the puzzle's own opening dice roll
finishes settling.

**Why:** rating a puzzle's difficulty by feel alone doesn't scale past a
handful of hand-picked examples, and the project can't calibrate those
five numbers against how a real puzzle actually plays without a precise,
move-by-move timing record to compare them against. This is the
measurement groundwork the thirty-candidate playtest (above) and its
eventual difficulty bands are built on.

## [infra] The project's visual-regression check moves onto a single, shared renderer

**What:** Up to now, the screenshots this project compares against to
catch an accidental visual regression were captured on whichever computer
happened to run the check, and different computers draw fonts and edges
slightly differently — enough to look like a change when nothing in the
game actually moved. Thirteen of the project's screenshot baselines were
re-captured using one single machine (the automated test runner) as the
one renderer whose output counts from now on, and nine of those thirteen
differ from the old baselines by more than this project's 2% notice
threshold, shown below. None of these differences are a change to the
game — they're the same screens, redrawn once by a different machine. A
new "ready" signal (the screen says when its fonts and layout have
actually settled) also replaced a flat timed wait before every capture,
which had been quietly outrunning fast animations like the puzzle board's
dice roll (see above).

**Why:** a flat wait before every screenshot is either too short (a
flaky, half-settled capture) or too long (a slow check on every single
automated change) — and a check run on a different machine each time
can never build a stable history to compare against. Picking one machine
as the permanent renderer of record, and waiting on a real signal instead
of a guessed delay, is what makes "did this screen actually change"
answerable going forward instead of noisy.

## [infra] A legibility check for the title screen's plate

**What:** A new automated check measures how readable the title screen's
wordmark and its small psalm line are against the dark plate they sit on
— four contrast measurements in total, checked against accessibility-style
minimum ratios. It runs as part of the project's normal art checks and
only fails if a plate gets installed that is both different from the
last approved one and too low-contrast to read comfortably. The currently
installed plate passes comfortably on its own merits (its lowest measured
ratio is nearly double the required minimum).

**Why:** the title screen's look is picked by the maintainer by eye, not
by an automated rule — this doesn't change that, but it means a future
plate pick gets an objective legibility check before it ships, rather than
relying on everyone remembering to squint at it on a phone.

## [infra] A plan that can no longer silently disagree with itself

**What:** The project tracks its upcoming deadlines in one table and its
build queue in another, and until now nothing checked that the two agreed
with each other. A new automated check now fails the project's own test
suite if a deadline item has no matching row in the build queue, claims a
queue row that doesn't exist, double-books one row under two different
deadlines, or sits outside the date window it claims to be in — and it
names exactly which row and which file disagree, rather than leaving a
person to notice by hand.

**Why:** a deadline plan nobody is checking for internal consistency is a
plan that quietly drifts out of date exactly when it matters most — right
before something is due. This closes that gap with a hard failure instead
of a hope that someone notices.

## [infra] Two copies of the same note no longer turn into a silent conflict

**What:** When two separate branches of work each bump the same lesson
("we hit this problem again") or the same "needs a person" note at the
same time, the tool that merges them together (git) used to just keep
both copies side by side with no warning — which quietly broke the
project's own lint rules on the receiving branch, six times in two days,
each one undone and fixed by hand. A new merge rule now combines two
bumps of the same note into one row automatically — keeping the higher
repeat count, the latest date, and the union of everything both copies
cited — and only gives up (leaving an ordinary, visible conflict) when
the two copies genuinely disagree in a way that can't be safely combined.

**Why:** six silent breaks in two days, three of them on one single pull
request, is exactly the kind of recurring paper cut this project's own
process keeps a running ladder of — once a problem repeats enough times
by hand, it earns an automatic fix instead of a repeated manual one.

**Commits:** `7745d4dae` (phase ML1a; PR #695)

**No capture:** a git-merge rule for the project's own planning
documents; no in-game screen.

## [infra] A private notebook for brainstorming now has a safe bridge into the project

**What:** The maintainer's personal brainstorming notes — goals, a map of
ideas, a running inbox — now live in their own small, separate, private
repository, with a script that safely pulls the latest version into the
main project folder (and refuses to do anything risky, like merging over
local changes, if anything looks out of the ordinary) and a new `/vault`

**Why:** ideas the maintainer jots down away from a keyboard need a safe,
low-ceremony path into the place where they actually get turned into
planned work, without risking the notebook and the project's own history
getting tangled together by an ordinary sync.
