## Scenes

### S-01 · `bw-w1` Windmill · stop 1 (plain) · speaks: Ann Greave

1. Before the roads there was one lamp over all this country. It hung from a chain of four hundred links and burned without oil or tending, and whatever stood beneath it cast no shadow, neither man nor beast nor standing stone. I know the count of the links. The shadows it forbade did not perish. They went into the sea, and the folk of this coast call them the Owed.

2. Then the lamp was let down and broken, and the pieces were carried off like the relics of a murdered saint to the houses of those who could pay. You come to the coast in the age that followed, on foot, through fields gone to thistle and dock and the grey stalks of a grain no one has sown in living memory. The sea beyond is black from shore to rim.

3. A windmill stands on the headland with its sails lashed and its stones still. Below it lies a walled harbour built against a reef that curves out of the surf pale and toothed, and on the far head a tower with no fire in it. A woman waits in the door of the mill.

4. Ann Greave says, You are going down. Am I wrong. I am not. A boat of ours did not come in last night and my brother is in her, and who in that town has an hour to walk the shore and look. No one. So it is you. She looks at your hands and then at the road. Find her men, she says, and she does not say it twice.

### S-02 · `bw-w2` Gallows crossroads · stop 2 (hard)

1. At the crossroads stands a frame of grey oak with an iron cresset hung from its arm, to bring walkers in off the heath. When the fires failed the walkers yet came, in snow and in fog, and they lie under the cairns along the ditch where the frost found them.

2. Their shadows did not lie down with them. On a clear night you may see those yet upon the heath, thirty one, walking in file toward a fire that is not there.

### S-03 · `bw-h1` Quay end · stop 1 (plain)

1. After dusk the harbour goes on working. Men coil line by the feel of it and step from gunwale to gunwale with no lantern among them. You go down among the tarred hulls and the stacked creels and the nets hung like the cerements of some drowned order, and no one calls out. Beyond the mole the sea heaves under the stars and gives none of them back.

### S-04 · `bw-h3` Harbour light pier · stop 3 (cruel)

1. On the pier stands an iron post with a fire pan at its head, where a flame was kindled each dusk from the lamp in the tower. Thirty years gone the council sat one evening and sold that lamp to the lord of the keep by a show of hands. Each house that raised a hand was paid in coin, and no word of it was sent to the ships.

2. That night the sea came up the harbour steps as far as the third stair and stood there until morning, black and without wave, and went down again.

### S-05 · `bw-h4` The wreck · stop 1 (plain)

1. She lies on the outer reef with her back broken across it. At the ebb you can see the sockets, each wider than a well, and the boat lies in the gap where a tooth is gone, and the swell goes in and out of her like breath. Her nets are aboard and her oars are shipped and there is no one in her.

2. Whatever owned this jaw was dead before the sea was salt. The town calls it the Gape and fishes in its lee.

### S-06 · `bw-h5` Moored ship · stop 4 (cruelty at 11)

1. A hulk lies chained at the inner mole and in her great cabin the town keeps its book. Each wreck has a page. The shares are set down by household, and the custom is written at the head of every leaf, that no swimmer is to be taken from the water until the cargo is in.

2. They hold to it. When the cargo is ashore the children are let down among the drowned before any grown man, that they may have first choice of the boots, and the mothers stand at the rail and call down which to take. The drowned, when the boots are off them, are rowed by night to the sea door of the keep. The book calls this the tithe and has a column for it.

### S-07 · `bw-c0` Cave face · stop 1 (plain)

1. The cave lets you out beneath the cliff into a cove no boat could find. The water lies still as poured lead. Where the swell turns the weed a cold fire wakes in it and runs and goes out, green and without heat, and by that light you see your own shadow on the shingle. It lies as a shadow should. You stand a while and look at it.

### S-08 · `bw-c2` Fishermen's camp · stop 5 (grief) · speaks: Abel Corse

1. Seven men sit about a fire of wreckwood far back under the overhang, where the sea cannot see it, and the eighth sits apart with his back to it. That is Abel Corse, who was master of the boat. He does not turn.

2. Abel Corse says, Wind north and east, backing. Nine fathom on the lead and shoaling. Forty years the master has brought her in by the feel of the bottom. That night there was a light on the head, white, and nothing in the boat cast a shadow by it, and the master put the helm over and stood in for it like a man going home.

3. He says, The boy was in the bow to call the rocks. Eleven years old, the master's daughter's son. He called them. He called them twice. The master said, Hush, it is lit. The sea gave the boy back on the second tide and the men put him under stones above the wrack, and they keep a watch on the stones.

4. He has the boy's bow line in his lap. He has coiled it and shaken it out and coiled it again since they came ashore, and he is coiling it now.

### S-09 · `bw-t2` Bridge approach · stop 1 (plain)

1. The river comes down to the sea east of the town and one bridge crosses it, with a chain drawn across at the height of your chest. On the far bank the country lies low and wooded and a smoke stands over it from end to end like a second and lower sky. No bird crosses in either direction. The water runs clear to midstream and beyond midstream it runs grey, and the two do not mingle.

### S-10 · `bw-t4` Harbour town · stop 2 (hard) · speaks: Hob Lathe, Verity Scole, Osric Kell

1. The herring did not come this year nor the year before. What the nets bring up now is black and boneless and is put back with a prayer. The town sits to its supper behind closed shutters, and the bowls are shallow and the children quiet, and the old go early to bed that they may be hungry in their sleep and not in company.

2. Hob Lathe keeps the inn. He says very low, You will want a bed. It is the quiet season. It has been the quiet season some while. He sets the bowl before you as if it might wake.

3. Verity Scole keeps the shop. She says, Salt, a peck, two shillings. Line, the fathom, a penny. Talk is a shilling the quarter hour and you have had a third of one. Across the lane Osric Kell stands at a cold forge and looks at the iron you carry. He says, Poor, and goes back in.

### S-11 · `bw-t6` Upper town · stop 1 (plain)

1. Above the lanes the houses are tall and built of dressed stone. Every window in the street looks west along the shore toward the keep, and in each a chair is set, and at evening the chairs are filled. There is not one pane in all that hill of glass that faces the sea.

### S-12 · `bw-l2` Clifftop chapel · stop 3 (cruel)

1. The chapel on the cliff was built to face the rising sun. The town drew it round on rollers in one summer to look upon the keep. The priest who would not carry the altar across was put out on the cliff road in January, and the one who came after was paid.

2. Where the altar lamp hung they have hung a glass float filled with seawater from under the keep, and at night it is very faintly white. They kneel beneath it to the lord of that house and call him the Saint Beneath.

### S-13 · `bw-l3` Cliff road · stop 1 (plain)

1. The road runs along the cliff top through thrift and short grass and the wind comes clean off the water. Ahead the tower stands unlit against the sky. It is a single bone, hollowed, and the stair winds up inside it where the marrow was. Two hundred and six steps to the lamp room. You did not ask. The seventh from the top is loose and was loose before any lamp was carried up it.

### S-14 · `bw-l5` Lighthouse · stop 4 (cruelty at 11) · speaks: Enoch Marrable

1. The warden of the tower is Enoch Marrable and he climbs it at dusk every night of the year. He trims no wick, for there is none. He stands his watch beside the empty mount and at dawn he comes down and writes in the log.

2. He turns the book that you may see it. Enoch Marrable says, Fourteenth of the month. Wind south west, fresh. Lit at the sixth hour, burned clear, doused at dawn. Thirty years and no entry missed. Remark the hand.

3. The town pays him four shillings a week to write it. Each quarter the log goes inland to the men who draw the charts, and the charts yet show a light on this head, and the masters of strange ships yet steer for it with their crews asleep below. He is proud of the hand.

### S-15 · `bw-w5` Keep causeway · stop 1 (plain)

1. A causeway of fitted stone runs out to the keep and the tide covers it twice in a day. Along the wrack line the town has left what it leaves. Loaves gone green, a bell with no tongue, a comb, a christening spoon.

2. Beyond these the Owed stand in the shallows in their hundreds, in the shapes of men and of cattle, facing the keep, and the tide goes through them.

### S-16 · `bw-k1` Keep gate · stop 5 (grief) · speaks: Serjeant Dunmore

1. The gate of the keep is oak banded in iron and a serjeant stands before it in a coat too heavy for the season. Serjeant Dunmore says, None to pass without the key. None to pass without the key. Those are the orders and I have them by heart.

2. There is a grate low in the gate. Each evening he kneels and puts bread through it and something on the other side takes the bread. His son went in at sixteen to serve the lord of the place. The boy's shadow came out again the same evening, alone, and it has stood at the foot of the serjeant's bed every night for four winters.

3. Dunmore says, He was a talker. You could not get your word in. None to pass without. He stops there. Behind the gate something breathes, slow and wet, and the serjeant brushes the crumbs from his knee.

### S-17 · `bw-k2` Sea keep · stop 1 (plain)

1. The stair goes down and not up. Under the keep is a round room with one door to the sea, shut now, and the lamp of the harbour burns on the bare floor, white and soundless. You look for your shadow and there is the floor and the light upon it.

2. The lord of the place sits beyond the lamp. Thirty years in that light have taken the edges from him, and he is grown pale and manifold as coral grows, branched and rebranched, and through him you can see the wall. About him on the floor lie the tithes of all those years, laid out in rows, and not one of them has been let to rot.

3. He turns toward you what serves him for a face. I have stood in that room. I will not tell you when.

### S-18 · `bw-d5` Old watchtower · stop 2 (hard)

1. The tower on the north head was manned against a fleet that was said to be coming. Six men and stores for a season. The relief was to follow in the spring. The log is still on the table and the entries go on for three years in a smaller and smaller hand, and the last of them say only fog, and fog, and wind north, and fog.

### S-19 · `bw-b1` River bridge · stop 1 (plain) · speaks: Tolliver Pease

1. At the bridge head a small man sits on a stool beside the chain with a pot of something warm. Tolliver Pease says, Sit, sit. You want across. Everyone wants across. What will you give me to lift it. No, keep it, I cannot be bought. I have been bought already and the man was prompt.

2. He says, While he sits in that house the chain stays drawn, and I would not cross if I were you, and I am not, thank God. Look at that smoke. Does that look like a country. He laughs and the river goes on under him in its two colours, bearing nothing.

### S-20 · `bw-p3` High pass · stop 3 (cruel)

1. The pass is the one road inland and there is a hut of stones at the top of it. In the hungry years families walked up from the coast with what they could carry. Men waited at the hut and asked a toll in coin of people who had only ever been paid in fish. Those who could not pay were relieved of their cooking pot. They turned back.

### S-21 · `bw-m5` Walled manor · stop 1 (plain)

1. The manor stands inside its own wall and there are candles in the windows, tallow, bought dear from inland. No one at the gate asks your name or your road. A fire is lit for you and you sit before it, and for one night the dark is a thing that stands outside a wall.

### S-22 · `bw-b2` Far bank · stop 4 (cruelty at 11)

1. From the far bank you look back. The keep is dark and the tower is dark. What you carried out of that room is a splinter no longer than your thumb, white and cold to hold. The town has watched you go.

2. At nightfall they come out along the cliff with timber and pitch. You watch them build the fire with their own hands on the head above the reef. Then they go down to the rail and stand in a row with their children before them and wait, and someone has brought the book. Beyond the reef the black water has begun to move toward the fire.

3. I have seen it done on other coasts. Inland a mountain you had taken for cloud shows one point of fire near its summit, and it was not there at dusk.

## Quest chain

### C-01 · step 1 · The Boat on the Rocks

- **Quest log:** A town boat went onto the outer reef in the night. Her men are not aboard and the town will not look. Find them.
- **Given:** Ann Greave, in the windmill scene.
- **Ends:** Abel Corse says, Seven living. Tell her that her brother is one of the seven. Tell her the master is not.

### C-02 · step 2 · The Light He Saw

- **Quest log:** Abel Corse steered for a light on the head. No lamp has burned in that tower for thirty years. Ask its warden who lit it.
- **Given:** Abel Corse says, Ask the man in the tower who lit it. The master would know that much.
- **Ends:** Enoch Marrable says, Entered as lit. It was not lit. Your man saw the sea door under the keep, standing open, and the light of it laid along the head. They paid me a key when they took the lamp. I have not used it.

### C-03 · step 3 · The Paid Key

- **Quest log:** No one lit the tower. A sea door stands open under the keep on certain nights, and the light is there. The key Marrable was paid fits the keep gate.
- **Given:** Enoch Marrable says, Take it. I will enter it as lost overboard.
- **Ends:** Serjeant Dunmore says, None to pass without the key. That is the key. Then those are the orders. If he knows you in there, tell him. No. Tell him nothing. Pass.

### C-04 · step 4 · The Saint Beneath

- **Quest log:** The lamp of the harbour burns under the keep, and the lord who bought it sits beside it. Go down.
- **Given:** The key in the gate.
- **Ends:** Tolliver Pease says, Well. He is done, then. I heard it in the chain, it has gone slack as a widow's purse. Cross. No charge. There is no one left to charge it to.

Step 4 is turned in at the river bridge (`bw-b1`), as the engine has it.

## Refusals

- **R-01** · `bw-c2` Fishermen's camp: The men by the fire look past you. You were not sent, and they have nothing for one who was not sent.
- **R-02** · `bw-l5` Lighthouse: Enoch Marrable is at his log and does not lift his head. He has no word for one who brings him no question.
- **R-03** · `bw-k1` Keep gate: The serjeant does not move from the gate. None to pass without the key, he says, and you have no key.
- **R-04** · `bw-b1` River bridge: Tolliver Pease pats the chain as he would a dog. Not while he sits in that house, he says, and offers you the stool.

## Portrait prompts

For T's own image generation; none is in the game. Each is used with the shared style block.

**Style block.** Full-figure standing character, centred, feet visible, on a pure black background. Flat inked illustration in the manner of an old dungeon-crawler monster manual: heavy black brush linework, solid spot-black shadows, rough woodcut hatching, ragged distressed edges. Limited muted palette: one dominant garment colour, bone-white skin, and one small accent. No gradients, no scenery, no ground, no text, no border. The face is grotesque and a little caricatured, the proportions slightly wrong. Dark, strange and imaginative, never cute, never glossy, never anime, never 3D. Portrait format, 2:3.

- **Ann Greave** (The miller's widow; dominant colour: dull ochre). A tall gaunt woman, a miller's widow on a storm coast. Her arms are white with flour to the elbow, as if dipped. She wears an apron cut from an old sail, with the reef points still hanging from it, and a cap made of a gull's wing bound flat to her skull. She leans on a broken windmill sail spar taller than she is. A string of millstone chips hangs at her throat. Her eyes are pale and dusted and fixed on the viewer, and her mouth is set as if she has already heard the answer.
- **Abel Corse** (Master of the lost boat; dominant colour: sea green). An old fisherman who has been in the water too long. Barnacles grow in a band down one side of his face and along one forearm. One eye is milk white. His hair and beard hang like wet weed. He wears a torn oilskin and is barefoot. In both hands he coils a wet rope whose other end runs up inside his own sleeve. Lead sounding weights hang from his belt. He looks down at the rope and not at the viewer.
- **Enoch Marrable** (Keeper of the dark lighthouse; dominant colour: Prussian blue). A very tall, very thin lighthouse keeper in a long greatcoat with tarnished brass buttons. On his head, in place of a hat, he wears an iron lantern cage with nothing inside it. Small round spectacles with smoked black lenses. His fingers are stained ink black to the second knuckle. A huge logbook hangs open from a chain at his waist, and he holds a quill upright like a candle. His posture is proud and exact.
- **Serjeant Dunmore** (Warden of the keep gate; dominant colour: oxblood). A broad, tired serjeant in a rusted breastplate worn over a coat far too heavy. The visor of his helmet has rusted shut over one eye. He leans on a halberd as on a crutch. The knees of his hose are worn through and white from kneeling. A great iron ring hangs at his belt with no keys on it. In his free hand he holds half a loaf, and there are crumbs down his front.
- **Tolliver Pease** (The bridge-keeper; dominant colour: mustard yellow). A small round man seated on a low stool, grinning with too many small even teeth. His coat is knotted from toll ropes and lengths of chain. His wide hat is sewn all over with coins that overlap like fish scales. One arm is far too long and rests along a heavy chain that runs out of the picture. In the other hand he holds a steaming pot. He looks delighted to see the viewer.
- **Hob Lathe** (The innkeeper; dominant colour: faded plum). A huge, soft, hairless innkeeper, pale as tallow, with eyes almost closed. One thick finger is raised to his lips. He wears an apron hung with dozens of room keys, and from each key dangles a small tag of the kind tied to a drowned man's toe. An unlit candle stub is tucked behind his ear. He wears felt slippers and stands very quietly.
- **Verity Scole** (The shopkeeper; dominant colour: rust red). A thin sharp woman buried under layers of coats that belonged to other people, all different sizes. A wooden yoke lies across her shoulders and a dozen pairs of sea boots hang from it by their laces. In one hand she holds an abacus strung with knucklebones, in the other a small pair of scales. A veil hides her chin and mouth. Her eyes are counting.
- **Osric Kell** (The smith; dominant colour: slate violet). A massive smith whose bald head and shoulders are grey and pitted like slag. A scorched leather apron. Both forearms are wound in heavy chain that has been welded shut around them and has sunk into the flesh as into a tree. An iron guard covers his jaw and mouth. He holds a hammer whose head is forged in the shape of a single great chain link. He does not look at the viewer.
- **The Keeper** (The narrator, the last lamplighter; dominant colour: bone and soot, one cold white accent). A gaunt old lamplighter in a long soot-black coat. He carries a lamplighter's pole with a snuffer hook at the top and no flame. A fine chain of hundreds of links is looped over his shoulder again and again until it is as thick as a hawser. A wide hat throws his face into solid black shadow, and only a thin patient smile shows. A ledger is clamped under his arm. One small cold white glow shows between the fingers of his closed hand. He casts no shadow of any kind.
